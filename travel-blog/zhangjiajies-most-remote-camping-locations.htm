Zhangjiajie’s Most Remote Camping Locations

Home / Travel Blog / Blog Details

Zhangjiajie, a name that conjures images of towering quartzite sandstone pillars shrouded in mist, is one of China’s most iconic natural wonders. While most tourists flock to the glass bridges, cable cars, and well-trodden paths of the Zhangjiajie National Forest Park, a growing tribe of adventurers is seeking something far more raw and immersive: remote camping. These are not your average car-camping spots with picnic tables and fire rings. These are locations that require hours of hiking, a tolerance for solitude, and a willingness to sleep under the shadow of peaks that inspired the floating mountains in Avatar. If you are looking to escape the selfie sticks and the endless chatter of tour groups, this guide to Zhangjiajie’s most remote camping locations is your ticket to a truly unforgettable wilderness experience.

Why Camp Remote in Zhangjiajie?

Before we dive into the specific locations, it’s worth understanding why you would want to trade a comfortable hotel bed for a sleeping bag on a rocky outcrop. Zhangjiajie’s core scenic areas are notoriously crowded. During peak seasons, the “Avatar Hallelujah Mountain” viewpoint can feel more like a subway platform than a serene natural wonder. Remote camping offers a stark contrast. You get the sunrise over the pillars without a hundred other cameras clicking. You hear the wind whistle through the karst spires instead of the drone of a tour guide’s loudspeaker. Moreover, the region’s unique microclimates and biodiversity—from wild macaques to rare orchids—are best experienced in the quiet hours of dawn and dusk. Camping deep in the backcountry also allows you to access areas that day-trippers simply cannot reach, offering a perspective that is both humbling and exhilarating.

Essential Gear and Legal Considerations

Camping in Zhangjiajie is not like camping in a national park in the United States. The rules are stricter, and the terrain is more demanding. First, you need a permit. Unauthorized camping is technically illegal in many parts of the national forest park, but designated backcountry camping areas or “wild camping” zones with local guide approval are your best bet. Hiring a local guide is not just recommended; it is often mandatory for safety and legal reasons. They know the patrol schedules, the water sources, and the spots where rangers are more lenient.

Your gear list should include a lightweight tent with a robust rainfly (rain is frequent and sudden), a high-quality sleeping bag rated for 40°F (5°C) or lower, a portable water filter or purification tablets (streams are abundant but not always clean), and a reliable headlamp. Do not forget insect repellent—the mosquitoes in the subtropical forests are relentless. Also, pack out every single piece of trash. The Chinese tourism authorities are increasingly cracking down on littering, and leaving no trace is the only way to keep these remote spots open for future adventurers.

Location 1: The Hidden Valley of Tianzi Mountain

Tianzi Mountain, or “Son of Heaven Mountain,” is famous for its sheer cliffs and panoramic views, but most visitors only see the top via cable car. The real magic lies in the valleys below. One of the most remote camping spots is a small, unnamed plateau located about a 4-hour hike off the main trail from the Tianzi Mountain Scenic Area. This location is not marked on any official map. You will need a guide to navigate the overgrown paths and occasional landslide debris.

Why Camp Here? The plateau offers a 270-degree view of the surrounding pillars, and at sunset, the rock formations turn a deep, fiery orange. The campsite is situated near a small, cold stream, which is perfect for refilling water bottles. The isolation is profound. You will hear nothing but the rustle of leaves and the distant call of birds. The biggest challenge here is the wind. The plateau is exposed, so you will need to stake your tent down securely. Bring a windproof jacket and a hot meal. This is a spot for experienced campers who want to feel completely disconnected from the modern world.

Pro Tip: Arrive at the trailhead by 7 AM to avoid park rangers who might question your overnight gear. Your guide will likely know a back entrance that bypasses the main ticket gate.

Location 2: The Secret Beach of Baofeng Lake

Baofeng Lake is a popular tourist destination known for its emerald-green waters and boat rides. But few know that the lake has a hidden arm that extends deep into the forest, accessible only by a narrow, unmaintained trail. This leads to a small, sandy “beach” area that is perfect for a lakeside camp. The water here is surprisingly clear, and the surrounding cliffs create a natural amphitheater that amplifies the sounds of nature.

Why Camp Here? This is the easiest remote camping location on this list. The hike is only about 90 minutes from the main lake area, making it accessible for moderately fit campers. The campsite is flat and sandy, which is a luxury in the rocky terrain of Zhangjiajie. You can swim in the lake (though the water is chilly), and the bioluminescent plankton in the water create a magical glow on moonless nights. The downside is that you might not be completely alone. Other adventurous campers occasionally find this spot, but it is still far less crowded than the main lake area.

Safety Note: The trail to the secret beach is slippery after rain. Wear sturdy hiking boots with good grip. Also, be aware of wild boars that come to the lake to drink at night. Store your food in a bear-proof canister or hang it from a tree branch at least 10 feet off the ground.

Location 3: The Ridge of the Sleeping Dragon

The Sleeping Dragon Ridge is a long, narrow spine of rock that connects two larger mountain systems in the western part of the Wulingyuan Scenic Area. This is not a place for the faint of heart. The trail to the ridge is a grueling 6-hour hike that involves scrambling over boulders and traversing narrow ledges with sheer drops on either side. The campsite itself is a small, flat area on the ridge itself, barely large enough for two tents.

Why Camp Here? The view is unparalleled. You are literally sleeping on top of the world. The sunrise from the Sleeping Dragon Ridge is arguably the best in all of Zhangjiajie. The mist rolls in from the valleys below, and the pillars emerge like islands in a sea of clouds. The experience is deeply spiritual, almost otherworldly. However, this location is only for experienced climbers and campers. The ridge is extremely exposed to lightning, so you must check the weather forecast obsessively. If there is any chance of a thunderstorm, do not attempt this camp.

Logistics: You will need a specialized guide who is familiar with rock climbing techniques. Ropes and harnesses may be required for certain sections of the trail. Most tour operators in Zhangjiajie will not offer this trip due to the liability, so you will need to seek out independent guides, often found through local climbing communities or forums like Mafengwo (a Chinese travel platform). The cost is higher, but the reward is a camping experience that fewer than 100 people have ever done.

Location 4: The Abandoned Village of Laowuchang

Deep in the mountains, about 3 hours by car from the main Zhangjiajie city center, lies the abandoned village of Laowuchang. This was once a thriving Tujia ethnic village, but it was abandoned in the 1990s due to a landslide risk. Today, the stone houses are crumbling, and the terraced fields are overgrown with wild grass. It is a hauntingly beautiful place, and it offers something unique: the chance to camp inside a ruined structure.

Why Camp Here? Camping inside a stone house provides shelter from the wind and rain, which is a huge advantage over exposed ridge camps. The village is surrounded by terraced fields that are now filled with wildflowers in the spring and golden grass in the autumn. You can explore the empty houses, find old farming tools, and imagine what life was like for the Tujia people. At night, the village is pitch black, and the stars are incredibly bright. This location is also a great base for day hikes to nearby waterfalls and caves.

Cultural Note: The Tujia people have a rich culture of song and dance. While the village is abandoned, the nearby active villages still practice these traditions. If you are lucky, your guide might arrange a visit to a local family for a meal of tu jia la rou (smoked pork) and corn wine. This is a cultural immersion that complements the camping experience.

Challenges: The village is not completely deserted. Local farmers occasionally pass through to check on their terraced fields. You should be respectful and ask permission if you want to camp in a specific house. Also, the area is known for its population of venomous snakes, including the Chinese cobra. Always check your sleeping bag and boots before using them, and keep your tent zipped at all times.

Location 5: The Canyon of the Golden Whip Stream (Upper Reaches)

The Golden Whip Stream is one of the most popular hiking trails in Zhangjiajie, but the crowds thin out dramatically once you pass the halfway point. The upper reaches of the stream, where the water cascades over smooth rocks and the canyon narrows to just a few meters wide, are almost entirely ignored by tourists. This is where you can find a series of small, gravel bars that are perfect for camping.

Why Camp Here? The sound of running water is incredibly soothing, and the canyon walls provide natural insulation from the wind. This is a great spot for a relaxing camp where you can swim in the stream, fish for small trout (catch and release only), and simply listen to the forest. The upper reaches are also home to a family of wild macaques that are curious but generally harmless if you do not feed them.

Important Rule: Do not camp directly on the stream bank during the rainy season (May to August). Flash floods can occur with little warning. Always check the weather forecast for the entire upstream watershed, not just your immediate location. A sudden rainstorm miles away can send a wall of water down the canyon. Camp on a raised gravel bar that is at least 3 feet above the current water level.

Access: The upper reaches of the Golden Whip Stream are accessible from the back entrance of the national forest park, near the Suoxiyu area. You will need to arrange transportation with your guide, as public buses do not run this route. The hike from the trailhead to the best camping spots takes about 2 hours.

The Ethical Camper’s Code in Zhangjiajie

Remote camping in Zhangjiajie is a privilege, not a right. The Chinese government has been slowly restricting access to wild areas due to environmental damage and safety concerns. As a foreign or domestic traveler, you are an ambassador for the camping community. Follow these rules without exception:

  • Pack it in, pack it out. This includes all food scraps, toilet paper, and even biodegradable items like apple cores. The ecosystem is fragile, and animals that eat human food become dependent and aggressive.
  • Use established fire rings or stoves. Open fires are often banned in the dry season. Use a camping stove for cooking. If you must have a fire, keep it small and use only dead, fallen wood.
  • Respect wildlife. Do not approach or feed animals. Observe from a distance. If a macaque approaches your camp, make loud noises to scare it away rather than offering food.
  • Minimize noise. The whole point of remote camping is to enjoy the silence. Loud music or shouting ruins the experience for others and disturbs the wildlife.
  • Leave no trace of your campsite. When you leave, the site should look exactly as it did when you arrived. Scatter any rocks you moved, and brush away any footprints.

How to Plan Your Trip

Planning a remote camping trip in Zhangjiajie requires more preparation than a standard visit. Here is a step-by-step approach:

  1. Hire a Guide: This is non-negotiable for most remote locations. Look for guides on platforms like Mafengwo or through local hostels in Zhangjiajie city. Ask for references and check recent reviews. A good guide will handle permits, transportation, and navigation.
  2. Check the Season: The best time for remote camping is from September to November (autumn) and March to May (spring). Summer is hot, humid, and rainy, with frequent thunderstorms. Winter is cold and often snowy, but the views can be spectacular if you are prepared for sub-zero temperatures.
  3. Get the Right Permits: Your guide should obtain a “backcountry camping permit” from the local forestry bureau. This is a bureaucratic process, but it is essential to avoid fines or being escorted out of the park.
  4. Prepare for the Unexpected: Cell phone reception is spotty or non-existent in remote areas. Bring a satellite messenger or a personal locator beacon for emergencies. Also, have a backup plan in case your chosen campsite is occupied or inaccessible.
  5. Learn Basic Mandarin: While your guide will handle communication, knowing a few phrases like “wo yao zha ying di” (I want to camp) or “zhe li an quan ma” (Is this safe?) can be helpful. Most locals in the remote areas speak only the Tujia dialect or Mandarin.

The Magic of the Morning After

There is a moment that every remote camper in Zhangjiajie remembers. It is the moment just before sunrise, when the world is still gray and the mist hangs low. You unzip your tent, and the cold air hits your face. The smell of damp earth and pine fills your lungs. Then, the first ray of sunlight hits the top of the nearest pillar, and it glows like a candle. One by one, the other pillars catch the light, and the entire landscape transforms from a shadowy dream into a vivid, three-dimensional masterpiece. This is why you endure the difficult hike, the cold nights, and the bureaucratic hurdles. This is the Zhangjiajie that the day-trippers will never know.

The remote camping locations of Zhangjiajie are not for everyone. They demand physical fitness, mental resilience, and a deep respect for nature. But for those who answer the call, the reward is a connection to the land that is profound and lasting. Whether you choose the exposed ridge of the Sleeping Dragon, the haunted ruins of Laowuchang, or the hidden beach of Baofeng Lake, you will leave with more than just photographs. You will leave with a story that is uniquely yours, etched into the memory of the mountains themselves.
